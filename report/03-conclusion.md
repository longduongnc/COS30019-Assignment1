## 3. Conclusion and Recommendations

This report set out to assess how effective AI ethics guidelines and principles are in practice. Across the four key issues, the evidence supports the thesis that principles alone do little, and that their effect depends on what surrounds them.

First, guidelines influence awareness more than behaviour. Practitioners know that principles exist, but exposure to a code did not change decisions in a controlled study, and Australian developers interpreted the eight Principles inconsistently. Second, organisational incentives outweigh stated commitments. Ethics work competes with deadlines and revenue, and the 49-point gap between Australian organisations that claim alignment with the Principles and those that have implemented the practices shows how far statements and actions can drift apart. Third, part of the problem is that principles are abstract. Australia's move from value statements to six action-based practices is a well-directed response, although its effect has not yet been measured. Fourth, voluntary guidance has no consequences attached. Australia has chosen to rely on voluntary uptake and existing laws, which makes the effectiveness of its framework dependent on the weakest mechanism identified in the literature.

These findings do not support the view that ethics principles are useless. Principles set out what good practice looks like, give employees and the public a standard to appeal to, and provide the foundation on which practical guidance and regulation are built. Hagendorff's (2020) conclusion that guidelines "most often" have no impact is best read as a statement about principles used alone. The conclusion of this report is that ethics principles are necessary but not sufficient. They become effective when three conditions are met: they are translated into specific practices, those practices are tied to accountability and incentives inside organisations, and there are real consequences for ignoring them in high-risk settings.

Three recommendations follow from this.

1. **Organisations should make AI ethics someone's job.** A named senior owner, ethics criteria in project reviews, and recognition in performance measures address the incentive problems found in Section 2.2 and match the first practice of the Guidance.
2. **The Australian Government should measure whether the Guidance works.** An independent, repeated survey of adoption against the six practices, building on the Responsible AI Index, would show whether clearer guidance narrows the say-do gap.
3. **Binding requirements should apply where the risk of harm is high.** If voluntary uptake remains low, the mandatory guardrails proposed in 2024 should be reconsidered for high-risk uses, with voluntary guidance kept for lower-risk ones.
