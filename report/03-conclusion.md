## 3. Conclusion and Recommendations

This report asked how effective AI ethics guidelines and principles are in practice. My answer is that it depends on what you measure. Guidelines do raise awareness. Practitioners know about them, and most Australian organisations say their AI follows them. They do much less to change individual decisions, because developers interpret the principles differently and are left to handle trade-offs on their own. They do even less to change how organisations work, where deadlines and metrics usually win and where Australian organisations claim far more than they do (Figure 1). As for actual harm, almost nobody is measuring it, while recorded incidents keep going up.

Australia's move from value statements to six concrete practices answers the complaint that principles are too vague. But the practices it stresses most, accountability and human oversight, are the ones organisations are least likely to follow. By deciding in 2025 to rely on voluntary uptake and existing laws, the government has made its framework depend on the mechanism the evidence shows is weakest. The Productivity Commission is right that regulating processes does not guarantee good outcomes, but that applies even more to processes nobody has to follow.

This review has limits. A lot of the evidence is self-reported, most studies come from large English-speaking tech companies, and very few measure harm directly. What I say about the 2025 Guidance is also provisional, since it is too new to have been evaluated.

Even so, my conclusion is that principles are a necessary starting point, but they do not work as a control on their own. I make three recommendations.

1. **Organisations should make AI ethics someone's job.** A named senior person, ethics criteria in project reviews and credit in performance reviews would tackle the incentive problems in Section 2.2.
2. **The government should measure outcomes, not just self-reports.** The new AI Safety Institute could track incidents, complaints and audit results alongside the Responsible AI Index, so the say-do gap can be checked independently.
3. **Binding rules should apply where the risk is high and existing law falls short.** The gap analyses the Productivity Commission proposed should be finished quickly. Where they find gaps, the answer should be outcome-focused obligations, not more voluntary guidance.
