## 3. Conclusion and Recommendations

This report asked how effective AI ethics guidelines and principles are in practice. The answer depends on what is measured. Guidelines are effective at raising awareness: practitioners know them, and most Australian organisations say their AI aligns with them. They are much weaker at changing individual decisions, which are left to developers who interpret the principles differently and must resolve trade-offs alone. They are weaker again at changing organisational practice, where deadlines and metrics usually win, and where Australian organisations claim alignment far more often than they act on it (Figure 1). Their effect on actual harm is largely unmeasured, while recorded incidents keep rising.

Australia's shift from value statements to six concrete practices answers the criticism that principles are too abstract. Yet the practices it emphasises, especially accountability and human oversight, are the ones organisations implement least. By choosing in 2025 to rely on voluntary uptake and existing laws, the government has made the effectiveness of its framework depend on the mechanism the evidence shows to be weakest. The Productivity Commission is right that regulating processes does not guarantee outcomes, but the same argument applies even more strongly to processes that are voluntary.

This review has limits. Much of the evidence is self-reported, most studies come from large English-speaking technology firms, and very few measure harm directly. Conclusions about the 2025 Guidance are necessarily provisional, because it is too new to have been evaluated.

Even so, I conclude that principles are a necessary starting point rather than a working control. Three recommendations follow.

1. **Organisations should make AI ethics someone's job.** A named senior owner, ethics criteria in project reviews and recognition in performance measures address the incentive problems in Section 2.2 and put the Guidance's first practice into effect.
2. **The government should measure outcomes, not only self-reports.** The new AI Safety Institute could track incidents, complaints and audit results alongside the Responsible AI Index, so that the say-do gap can be checked independently.
3. **Binding requirements should apply where risk is high and existing law falls short.** The gap analyses proposed by the Productivity Commission should be completed quickly. Where they find gaps, outcome-focused obligations should follow, not more voluntary guidance.
