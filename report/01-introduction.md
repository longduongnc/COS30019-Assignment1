# Introduction — Draft (revised Week 5)

**Topic 6:** What is the effectiveness of ethics guidelines and principles in practice?

**Working thesis:** Ethics principles on their own change little. They start to matter when they are turned into specific practices, someone inside the organisation is accountable for them, and ignoring them has consequences in high-risk settings.

---

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background

AI now helps decide who gets hired, who gets a loan and who gets flagged by police, and ethics guidelines have multiplied alongside it. Jobin et al. (2019) found 84 of them worldwide. A few years later, Corrêa et al. (2023) reviewed 200 and found at least 17 principles that kept coming up. Australia published its own set in 2019. *Australia's AI Ethics Principles* are eight voluntary principles: human, societal and environmental wellbeing; human-centred values; fairness; privacy protection and security; reliability and safety; transparency and explainability; contestability; and accountability (Department of Industry, Science and Resources [DISR], 2019). In 2024 the government added a Voluntary AI Safety Standard with ten guardrails. In October 2025 the National AI Centre cut these down to six "essential practices" in the *Guidance for AI Adoption*: decide who is accountable, understand impacts and plan accordingly, measure and manage risks, share essential information, test and monitor, and maintain human control (DISR, 2025a).

So do any of these documents change what people actually do? Hagendorff (2020), after comparing 22 major guidelines, answers: "No, most often not" (p. 99). He points out that ethics has no way of enforcing itself, and that industry guidelines can even be used to argue that laws are unnecessary. Mittelstadt (2019) compares AI with medicine: doctors share professional goals, long-standing norms and real accountability, while AI developers lack all three. Floridi (2019) adds two risks: "ethics bluewashing", where organisations make misleading claims to look more ethical than they are, and "ethics shopping", where they pick whichever principles suit what they were already doing.

### 1.2 Key issues

Part of the problem is that "effective" can mean different things. A guideline could make people aware of an issue, change what an individual decides, change how an organisation works, or actually reduce harm. I will argue that the evidence is reasonably strong for the first and almost absent for the last. The report looks at four issues:

1. **Individual decision-making.** Do guidelines change how developers and practitioners behave?
2. **Organisational and economic pressures.** How do deadlines, metrics and commercial incentives affect whether principles are followed?
3. **The principles-to-practice gap.** Can abstract ideas like "fairness" be turned into concrete tools and processes, and does Australia's 2025 Guidance manage it?
4. **Voluntary versus binding approaches.** Can guidance work without enforcement, and what does Australia's recent policy decision mean?

My position is that principles on their own change little. They start to matter when they are turned into specific practices, when someone inside the organisation is accountable for them, and when ignoring them has consequences in high-risk settings.

### 1.3 Methodology and scope

This is a structured literature review. I started from the two base sources, S1 (DISR, 2019, 2025a) and S2 (Hagendorff, 2020), followed their citations in both directions, and searched Semantic Scholar, arXiv and the ACM Digital Library using terms such as "AI ethics guidelines effectiveness", "principles to practice" and "ethics washing". I gave priority to peer-reviewed studies of practitioners, and added Australian survey data, parliamentary and Productivity Commission reports, and the Stanford AI Index. I checked the details of academic sources against Crossref. An AI assistant, Claude (Anthropic, 2026), was used to search for sources, check references and draft text, as described in the Acknowledgements. Where sources disagree, I report both sides. The scope is guidelines for building and deploying AI, focusing on Australia. Australia makes a good case study because it has relied on voluntary principles since 2019 and has national survey data comparing what organisations claim with what they report doing. The EU AI Act is used only for comparison.
