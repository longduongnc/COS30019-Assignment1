# Introduction — Draft (revised Week 5)

**Topic 6:** What is the effectiveness of ethics guidelines and principles in practice?

**Working thesis:** Ethics principles on their own change little. They become effective only when they are translated into specific practices, tied to accountability inside organisations, and backed by consequences in high-risk settings.

---

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background

Over the past decade AI systems have moved into hiring, lending, healthcare and policing, and a wave of ethics guidelines has followed them. Jobin et al. (2019) counted 84 such documents worldwide, and a later review by Corrêa et al. (2023) analysed 200, identifying at least 17 recurring principles. Australia joined this movement in 2019 with *Australia's AI Ethics Principles*, eight voluntary principles: human, societal and environmental wellbeing; human-centred values; fairness; privacy protection and security; reliability and safety; transparency and explainability; contestability; and accountability (Department of Industry, Science and Resources [DISR], 2019). In 2024 the government added a Voluntary AI Safety Standard with ten guardrails, and in October 2025 the National AI Centre condensed these into the *Guidance for AI Adoption* and its six "essential practices": decide who is accountable, understand impacts and plan accordingly, measure and manage risks, share essential information, test and monitor, and maintain human control (DISR, 2025a).

The obvious question is whether any of this changes what people and organisations actually do. Hagendorff (2020), comparing 22 major guidelines, gives a blunt answer: "No, most often not" (p. 99). In his view ethics has no mechanism to enforce its own claims, and industry guidelines can even help companies argue that binding law is unnecessary. Mittelstadt (2019) adds that AI development, unlike medicine, lacks the shared goals, professional history and accountability structures that turn principles into practice. Floridi (2019) warns of "ethics bluewashing", making misleading claims in order to appear more ethical than one is, and "ethics shopping", choosing whichever principles justify what an organisation already does.

### 1.2 Key issues

"Effectiveness" can mean several different things. A guideline might raise awareness, change an individual's decisions, change an organisation's processes or, most importantly, reduce harm. This report argues that the evidence is strongest for the first of these and weakest for the last. It examines four key issues:

1. **Individual decision-making.** Do guidelines change how developers and practitioners behave?
2. **Organisational and economic pressures.** How do deadlines, metrics and commercial incentives affect whether principles are followed?
3. **The principles-to-practice gap.** Can abstract principles such as "fairness" be turned into concrete tools and processes, and does Australia's 2025 Guidance manage this?
4. **Voluntary versus binding approaches.** Can guidance work without enforcement, and what does Australia's recent policy choice imply?

My position is that principles alone change little. They become effective only when they are translated into specific practices, tied to accountability inside organisations, and backed by consequences in high-risk settings.

### 1.3 Methodology and scope

This report is a structured literature review. Starting from the two base sources, S1 (DISR, 2019, 2025a) and S2 (Hagendorff, 2020), I followed citations forwards and backwards and searched Semantic Scholar, arXiv and the ACM Digital Library for terms such as "AI ethics guidelines effectiveness", "principles to practice" and "ethics washing". Priority was given to peer-reviewed empirical studies of practitioners, supported by Australian national survey data, parliamentary and Productivity Commission reports, and the Stanford AI Index. Bibliographic details of academic sources were checked against Crossref. Evidence is weighed by its type and strength, and conflicting findings are reported rather than ignored. The scope is guidelines for developing and deploying AI, with a focus on Australia. Australia is a useful case because it has relied on voluntary principles since 2019 and has national survey data comparing what organisations claim with what they report doing. The EU AI Act is used only as a point of comparison.
