# Milestone 1 (Weeks 2–3): Introduction — Draft

**Topic 6:** What is the effectiveness of ethics guidelines and principles in practice?

**Working thesis:** Ethics principles on their own do little to change how AI is built and deployed. They become effective only when they are turned into concrete practices, backed by organisational incentives, and eventually supported by enforceable rules. Australia's move from its 2019 AI Ethics Principles to the practice-oriented *Guidance for AI Adoption* (2025) is a step in that direction. Because both remain voluntary, however, their real-world effectiveness is still uncertain.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background

Over the past decade, the rapid spread of artificial intelligence (AI) into areas such as hiring, lending, healthcare and policing has been accompanied by a wave of AI ethics guidelines. Governments, companies, professional bodies and research institutes have all published lists of principles that AI systems are supposed to follow. Jobin et al. (2019) identified 84 such documents worldwide. They found broad agreement on a small set of values: transparency, justice and fairness, non-maleficence, responsibility and privacy. Australia followed the same path. In 2019 the Department of Industry, Science and Resources released *Australia's AI Ethics Principles*, a set of eight voluntary principles: human, societal and environmental wellbeing; human-centred values; fairness; privacy protection and security; reliability and safety; transparency and explainability; contestability; and accountability (Department of Industry, Science and Resources [DISR], 2019). In October 2025, the National AI Centre published the *Guidance for AI Adoption*, which updates and simplifies the earlier Voluntary AI Safety Standard. It condenses the Standard's ten guardrails into six "essential practices": decide who is accountable, understand impacts and plan accordingly, measure and manage risks, share essential information, test and monitor, and maintain human control (DISR, 2025a).

This steady stream of principles raises a basic question: do they actually change what people and organisations do? Hagendorff (2020) compared 22 major guidelines and answered bluntly: "No, most often not" (p. 99). He argues that ethics lacks mechanisms to enforce its own claims. Guidelines can even serve industry by suggesting to legislators that self-regulation is enough and binding law is unnecessary. Others have made similar criticisms. Mittelstadt (2019) argues that, unlike medicine, AI development lacks shared goals, professional history, proven methods for turning principles into practice, and accountability mechanisms, so "principles alone cannot guarantee ethical AI". Floridi (2019) warns of "ethics washing" and "ethics shopping", where organisations use ethical language for reputation or pick the principles that justify what they already do.

### 1.2 Key issues

This report examines the effectiveness of AI ethics guidelines and principles in practice through four key issues:

1. **Impact on individual decision-making.** Is there evidence that guidelines change how developers and practitioners behave? A controlled study of 168 software engineering students and professionals found no statistically significant difference in ethical decisions between people who had seen the ACM Code of Ethics and people who had not (McNamara et al., 2018).
2. **Organisational and economic pressures.** How do commercial incentives, speed-to-market and competition shape whether principles are followed, and when does ethics become a public relations exercise rather than a constraint (Hagendorff, 2020; Floridi, 2019)?
3. **The "principles-to-practice" gap.** Abstract principles such as "fairness" or "transparency" are hard to act on. Can tools, methods and processes translate them into concrete engineering and governance decisions (Morley et al., 2020)? Australia's 2025 guidance, with its shift from values to actions, is an important case here.
4. **Voluntary versus binding approaches.** Can soft, non-binding guidance be effective without enforcement, or do principles only gain force once they are backed by regulation, audit or liability (Munn, 2023)?

### 1.3 Methodology and scope

This report is based on a structured literature review. The starting points are the two base sources: Australia's AI Ethics Principles together with the Guidance for AI Adoption (S1), and Hagendorff's (2020) evaluation of guidelines (S2). From these, sources were found by following citations forwards and backwards and by searching Google Scholar, the ACM Digital Library and Scopus. Search terms included "AI ethics guidelines effectiveness", "principles to practice", "ethics washing" and "responsible AI implementation". Priority is given to peer-reviewed empirical studies (surveys, interviews and controlled experiments with practitioners), supported by authoritative government and industry reports. Evidence is weighed by its type and strength, and conflicting findings are presented rather than ignored.

The scope is limited to ethics guidelines and principles that apply to the development and deployment of AI systems, with particular attention to the Australian context. Binding legislation such as the EU AI Act is discussed only as a point of comparison for voluntary approaches, not analysed in depth. Technical fairness and explainability methods are considered only as tools for putting principles into practice.
