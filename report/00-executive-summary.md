# Executive Summary — Draft

## Executive Summary

Governments, companies and professional bodies have published a large number of AI ethics guidelines, including Australia's AI Ethics Principles (2019) and the Guidance for AI Adoption (2025). This report asks whether such guidelines and principles are effective in practice, meaning whether they change how AI systems are actually built and used. It examines four key issues: whether guidelines influence the decisions of individual practitioners, how organisational and commercial pressures affect compliance, whether abstract principles can be translated into concrete practice, and whether voluntary guidance can work without enforcement.

The evidence shows that principles on their own have little effect on behaviour. Practitioners are generally aware of them, but a controlled experiment found that reading a code of ethics did not change decisions, and interviews show that developers interpret the same principle in different ways. Inside organisations, ethics work regularly loses to deadlines, metrics and revenue. In an Australian survey, 78% of executives said their AI aligned with the Principles while only 29% had implemented the matching practices. Australia's 2025 Guidance improves on the Principles by replacing value statements with six concrete actions, but it remains voluntary and has not yet been evaluated. The report concludes that ethics principles are necessary but not sufficient. They become effective when they are translated into specific practices, built into organisational incentives and accountability, and backed by enforceable requirements for high-risk uses. It recommends that organisations assign and reward responsibility for AI ethics, and that the Australian Government independently measure uptake of the Guidance and set binding requirements where the risk of harm is high.
