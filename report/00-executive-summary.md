# Executive Summary — Draft (revised Week 5)

## Executive Summary

Hundreds of AI ethics guidelines have been published by governments, companies and professional bodies, including Australia's AI Ethics Principles (2019) and the Guidance for AI Adoption (2025). This report asks whether these guidelines actually work, meaning whether they change how AI systems are built and used. It separates four levels of effectiveness (awareness, individual decisions, organisational practice and harm) and looks at four key issues: whether guidelines affect practitioners' decisions, how commercial pressure affects whether they are followed, whether abstract principles can be turned into concrete practice, and whether voluntary guidance can work without enforcement.

The evidence shows that guidelines raise awareness but rarely change behaviour by themselves. In a controlled experiment, reading a code of ethics made no difference to decisions. Interviews show developers reading the same principle in different ways, and studies inside tech companies find ethics work regularly losing to deadlines and metrics. In Australia, 78% of surveyed organisations said their AI aligned with the Principles, but only 29% had put matching practices in place, and the 2025 survey showed no improvement. The 2025 Guidance replaces values with six concrete actions, but its accountability and oversight practices are the least adopted. In December 2025 the government chose voluntary guidance and existing laws over mandatory guardrails. Meanwhile, recorded AI incidents worldwide rose from 233 in 2024 to 362 in 2025. The report concludes that principles are a necessary starting point but do not work as a control on their own. It recommends that organisations assign and reward accountability for AI ethics, that the government measure outcomes rather than self-reports, and that binding, outcome-focused rules apply where risk is high and existing law falls short.
