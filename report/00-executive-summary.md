# Executive Summary — Draft (revised Week 5)

## Executive Summary

Governments, companies and professional bodies have published hundreds of AI ethics guidelines, including Australia's AI Ethics Principles (2019) and the Guidance for AI Adoption (2025). This report asks whether such guidelines are effective in practice, meaning whether they change how AI systems are actually built and used. It distinguishes four levels of effectiveness (awareness, individual decisions, organisational practice and harm) and examines four key issues: whether guidelines influence practitioners' decisions, how commercial pressures affect compliance, whether abstract principles can be translated into concrete practice, and whether voluntary guidance can work without enforcement.

The evidence shows that guidelines raise awareness but rarely change behaviour on their own. A controlled experiment found that reading a code of ethics did not change decisions, interviews show that developers interpret the same principle in different ways, and studies inside technology companies find that ethics work regularly loses to deadlines and metrics. In Australia, 78% of surveyed organisations said their AI aligned with the Principles, but only 29% had implemented matching practices, and the 2025 survey showed no improvement. The 2025 Guidance replaces values with six concrete actions, but its accountability and oversight practices are the least adopted, and the government decided in December 2025 to rely on voluntary guidance and existing laws rather than mandatory guardrails. Meanwhile, recorded AI incidents worldwide rose from 233 in 2024 to 362 in 2025. The report concludes that principles are a necessary starting point but not a working control. It recommends that organisations assign and reward accountability for AI ethics, that the government measure outcomes rather than self-reports, and that binding, outcome-focused obligations apply where risk is high and existing law falls short.
