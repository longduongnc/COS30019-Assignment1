# Findings — Draft (revised Week 5)

**Topic 6:** What is the effectiveness of ethics guidelines and principles in practice?

---

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 Awareness without action

The simplest way to test a guideline is to see whether it changes what developers decide. McNamara et al. (2018) did exactly this. They gave 168 software engineering students and professionals a set of ethical scenarios, and showed half of them the ACM Code of Ethics first. On no scenario was there a statistically significant difference between the two groups. Hagendorff (2020) uses this result to say the effect of guidelines is "almost zero" (p. 108). I think that goes too far. The study used a general professional code rather than an AI-specific one, the scenarios were hypothetical, and participants read the code only once. What it really shows is that reading a code does not change decisions. It says little about guidelines built into training, reviews or management expectations.

Later studies suggest guidelines do reach practitioners, but then stall. Pant et al. (2024) surveyed 100 AI practitioners and found most were reasonably familiar with AI ethics, mostly through workplace rules and policies. They rated formal education as only somewhat helpful for putting it into practice. Interviews show why awareness is not enough. When Sanderson et al. (2023) asked 21 CSIRO scientists and engineers about Australia's eight Principles, the same principle meant different things to different people. For one participant fairness meant diverse training data, for another it meant non-discrimination. Transparency was usually treated as just explainability, and some participants pushed accountability away entirely: "Whether the [system] works in 10 years, it's not under our control" (Section IV-F). Holstein et al. (2019), drawing on 35 interviews and a survey of 267 practitioners, also found a gap between the fairness problems teams actually faced and the solutions researchers were offering.

Another problem is that principles pull against each other. Whittlestone et al. (2019) argue that they are "often too broad and high-level to guide ethics in practice" (p. 195), since they say nothing about what to do when accuracy and explainability conflict. The 2026 AI Index makes the same point with technical evidence. Improving privacy can reduce fairness, and improving safety can reduce accuracy, yet "there is no framework for navigating these trade-offs" (Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence [Stanford HAI], 2026, p. 126). So guidelines make people aware of the issues, but leave the hardest calls to individual developers.

### 2.2 Incentives over intentions

Why would people who know the principles not follow them? Most of the literature blames the organisation, not the individual. Hagendorff (2020) describes an industry where "speed is everything" and skipping ethics is "the path of least resistance" (p. 108). Research inside tech companies backs this up. Ali et al. (2023) studied the workers whose job is AI ethics and found that "policies, practices, and outcomes are decoupled" (p. 217). Ethics work loses out to launch deadlines, is hard to fit into the metrics that drive company goals, and gets disrupted every time teams are reorganised. Raising a concern becomes a personal risk. Rakova et al. (2021) interviewed 26 practitioners from 19 organisations. Many of those organisations only acted after something went wrong, usually negative media coverage, and their incentives often worked against responsible AI. In news reports, Google shut down its AI ethics council little more than a week after announcing it in 2019 (Piper, 2019), and Microsoft laid off its Ethics and Society team in 2023 (Schiffer & Newton, 2023). These are only anecdotes, but they show how quickly ethics teams can disappear.

The Australian data tells the same story. In the 2024 Responsible AI Index, 413 executives rated their AI against statements for each of the eight Principles and said which related practices they had put in place (Figure 1). On average 78% agreed their AI aligned with a principle, but only 29% had implemented the matching practice (Fifth Quadrant, 2024). The worst case was human-centred values: 82% claimed alignment, yet only 22% had assessed their AI's human rights risks.

A year later things had not improved. The 2025 Index surveyed 418 organisations against the Voluntary AI Safety Standard. The average score dropped from 44 to 43 out of 100, and only a third of organisations had adopted the Standard. Some of the least common practices were designated roles for responsible AI (25%) and oversight measures for autonomous systems (23%). This is despite 58% of organisations rating their own human control mechanisms highly (Fifth Quadrant, 2025). These surveys are self-reported and run by a commercial firm sponsored by the National AI Centre, so they need some caution. Still, people usually overstate their own good practice in surveys, which makes the gap more worrying, not less.

The same data does show where principles seem to help. The most mature organisations had put around 32 of 45 practices in place, and 84% of them had adopted the Principles, compared with 27% of the least mature (Fifth Quadrant, 2025). Global numbers look similar. In the AI Index's 2025 survey, the share of organisations with no responsible AI policy fell from 24% to 11%, and 8 percentage points more organisations than in 2024 said their policies had reduced AI incidents. At the same time, knowledge and training gaps, the most common obstacle, rose from 51% to 59% (Stanford HAI, 2026, p. 144). This is correlation, not proof of cause. It does match what Madaio et al. (2020) found when designing checklists with 48 practitioners: checklists can give ad hoc processes some "organizational infrastructure" (p. 1) and support the people pushing for change. Principles seem to work where the organisation already has the leadership and structure to act on them.

### 2.3 From principles to practices

A third explanation is that principles are simply too abstract. Morley et al. (2020) note that the debate has focused on the "what" of AI ethics rather than the "how". Lu et al. (2024), writing from CSIRO, are blunter: without guidance on best practice, "practitioners are left with nothing much beyond truisms" (p. 1). A scoping review by Sadek et al. (2025) concludes that responsible AI guidelines "currently fail to impact practice" (p. 199). It gives five reasons: the guidelines are abstract, values conflict, success is hard to measure, the development pipeline is fragmented, and nobody inside the organisation is clearly accountable.

Researchers have tried to fill this gap with tools. Raji et al. (2020) designed an internal audit process that checks decisions against an organisation's principles at each stage of development, and Lu et al. (2024) collected governance and design patterns. But the tools have their own weaknesses. Ayling and Chapman (2022) compared AI ethics tools with long-established impact assessment and audit methods from areas like environmental, privacy and financial regulation, and found clear gaps in auditing and risk assessment. Mökander et al. (2021) show that even well-designed ethics-based audits run into conceptual, technical, social, economic, organisational and institutional limits. In the end, a tool is only as good as an organisation's willingness to use it.

Australia has moved in this direction: the 2019 Principles state values, while the 2025 Guidance tells organisations what to do. Some of its six practices directly target the problems above. "Decide who is accountable" deals with the spread-out responsibility that Sanderson et al. (2023) and Sadek et al. (2025) describe, and "test and monitor" turns reliability into something that can actually be checked. The catch is that, according to the 2025 Index, these are exactly the practices organisations are least likely to follow. Only 25% had designated roles for responsible AI (Fifth Quadrant, 2025). The Guidance is better designed than the Principles, but there is no independent evaluation yet showing it changes behaviour, and clearer instructions do not fix the incentives described in Section 2.2.

### 2.4 Voluntary guidance and the limits of soft law

The last issue is whether voluntary guidance can work when nothing happens to those who ignore it. Munn (2023) goes as far as calling AI ethics principles "useless". In his view they are contested, cut off from an industry and education system that mostly ignores ethics, and "toothless", lacking consequences and adhering to "corporate agendas" (p. 869). Rességuier and Rodrigues (2020) see it differently. For them the problem is not ethics but its use "as a substitute for law", which "risks its abuse and misuse" (p. 1).

Australia's recent decisions show this debate playing out. In 2024 the government proposed mandatory guardrails for high-risk AI (DISR, 2024), and a Senate committee recommended "new, whole-of-economy, dedicated legislation to regulate high-risk uses of AI" (Senate Select Committee on Adopting Artificial Intelligence, 2024, Recommendation 1). Within a year this had been reversed. The Productivity Commission argued, first in an interim report and then in its final report, that AI-specific regulation should be "a last resort" (Productivity Commission, 2025, Recommendation 1.2). The National AI Plan released in December 2025 then decided to rely on existing, largely technology-neutral laws, voluntary guidance and a $30 million advisory AI Safety Institute (DISR, 2025b), as reported by ABC News (Evans, 2025). The European Union went the opposite way. Its AI Act places binding obligations on high-risk systems, with fines of up to 7% of global annual turnover for the most serious breaches (Regulation 2024/1689).

The Productivity Commission's argument deserves a serious answer. It said the guardrails "regulate processes – risk management, testing, and transparency – rather than outcomes and thus do not directly prevent harm" (Productivity Commission, 2025, Box 1.7). That is fair, and it fits with Ali et al.'s (2023) finding that policies and outcomes can drift apart. But the argument works against voluntary guidance even more. If required processes do not guarantee good outcomes, optional ones are even less likely to, especially when the Index shows most organisations are not following them. Existing law can also be slow. The Robodebt Royal Commission looked at an automated debt-recovery scheme that ran unlawfully for years before it was stopped. It recommended a consistent legal framework for automated decision-making in government (Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme, 2023, Recommendation 17.1).

On actual harm, the level that matters most, the evidence is thin. The AI Incident Database recorded 362 incidents in 2025, up from 233 in 2024, and average transparency among leading foundation model developers fell from 58 to 40 out of 100 (Stanford HAI, 2026). Some of the rise comes from AI being used more widely and incidents being reported better, so this does not prove guidelines have failed. It does show that a decade of principles has not led to any measurable drop in harm, and that no one is systematically checking whether guidelines reduce harm at all.

None of this makes principles useless. Bietti (2020) warns that attacking "ethics washing" can turn into "ethics bashing", treating ethics as nothing more than a corporate cover story. The agreement that Jobin et al. (2019) and Corrêa et al. (2023) found across guidelines has given regulators a shared language. The EU AI Act openly builds on the seven non-binding principles of the EU's 2019 Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI (Regulation 2024/1689, Recital 27). What seems to change behaviour, though, is when principles become binding. In the AI Index survey, the GDPR was the most cited influence on organisations' responsible AI practices (60%), while only 16% mentioned the voluntary OECD AI Principles (Stanford HAI, 2026, p. 146). So principles are necessary, but not enough. They describe what good practice looks like, but they only change behaviour reliably when organisations have a real reason to follow them.
