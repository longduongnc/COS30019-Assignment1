# Milestone 2 (Weeks 3–4): Findings — Draft

**Topic 6:** What is the effectiveness of ethics guidelines and principles in practice?

---

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 Guidelines raise awareness but do not decide behaviour

The most direct test of effectiveness is whether a guideline changes the choices of the people who build AI systems. The best-known experiment suggests it does not. McNamara et al. (2018) gave 63 software engineering students and 105 professional developers a set of ethical scenarios, showing only half of them the ACM Code of Ethics. They found no statistically significant difference between the two groups on any scenario. Hagendorff (2020) relies heavily on this result to conclude that the effectiveness of guidelines is "almost zero" (p. 108). The study has limits, however. It tested a general professional code rather than an AI-specific one, it used hypothetical vignettes rather than real projects, and participants saw the code only once. It shows that reading a code does not change decisions. It does not show that guidelines embedded in training, review processes or management expectations have no effect.

Later studies of AI practitioners give a more mixed picture. In a survey of 100 AI practitioners, Pant et al. (2024) found that most were reasonably familiar with AI ethics, and that this familiarity came mainly from workplace rules and policies. Guidelines therefore do reach practitioners. What happens next is less encouraging. Sanderson et al. (2023) interviewed 21 scientists and engineers at CSIRO about how their work aligned with Australia's eight AI Ethics Principles. Participants interpreted the same principle in different ways. Fairness meant diverse training data to one person and non-discrimination to another, and transparency was usually reduced to explainability. Contestability was hard to implement because design assumptions could not easily be revisited. Accountability was sometimes disclaimed altogether, with one participant saying of a deployed system, "Whether the [system] works in 10 years, it's not under our control" (Section IV-F). The authors conclude that the principles are "more focused on end users and people affected by AI technologies, rather than developers" (Section I), which leaves developers to resolve trade-offs on their own.

Taken together, this evidence suggests that guidelines succeed at the first step, awareness, and fail at the second, action. Practitioners know the principles exist, but the principles do not tell them what to do when accuracy conflicts with explainability or privacy with transparency.

### 2.2 Organisational incentives outweigh stated principles

If individuals are aware of principles but do not act on them, the next question is what stops them. The literature consistently points to the organisation rather than the person. Hagendorff (2020) argues that AI development follows an economic logic in which "speed is everything" and skipping ethical considerations is "the path of least resistance" (p. 108). Empirical work inside technology companies supports this. Ali et al. (2023) studied workers responsible for integrating AI ethics into products and found an environment where "policies, practices, and outcomes are decoupled" (p. 217). Ethics work struggled against product launch deadlines, was hard to express in the metrics that drive company goals, and was disrupted by frequent reorganisations. As a result, raising an ethical concern became a personal risk for the individual rather than a normal part of the job. Rakova et al. (2021) reached a similar conclusion from 26 interviews across 19 organisations. Most organisations acted on responsible AI only reactively, typically after negative media attention, and performance incentives rewarded revenue and shipping rather than harm prevention.

Events in industry are consistent with these findings. Media reports describe Google dissolving its external AI ethics council in April 2019, little more than a week after announcing it (Piper, 2019), and Microsoft laying off its Ethics and Society team in March 2023 while accelerating the release of generative AI products (Schiffer & Newton, 2023). These are journalistic accounts rather than scholarly evidence, but they illustrate how quickly ethics structures can be removed when they conflict with commercial priorities.

Australian data shows the same gap at scale. The Australian Responsible AI Index 2024 surveyed 413 executives responsible for AI in their organisations. On average, 78% agreed that their AI systems aligned with Australia's AI Ethics Principles, yet only 29% had implemented the corresponding practices. Organisations had adopted an average of 12 out of 38 responsible AI practices, and the mean index score was 44 out of 100 (Fifth Quadrant, 2024). The report itself calls this a "say-do" gap (p. 4). This is self-reported survey data from a commercial research firm, sponsored by the National AI Centre, so it should be read with some caution. If anything, though, self-reporting would be expected to overstate good practice, which makes the gap more striking.

There is some evidence on the other side. The same Index found that two thirds of organisations were aware of the Principles, and that the most mature organisations, which had adopted 28 practices on average, were those where senior leaders drove AI strategy. Hagendorff (2020) also acknowledges cases such as Google's withdrawal from Project Maven after employee protests. Principles can therefore work as a shared standard that employees and the public use to hold an organisation to account. Their effect depends on leadership and incentives, however, and not on the principles themselves.

### 2.3 The principles-to-practice gap and Australia's response

A third explanation for weak effectiveness is that principles are too abstract to act on. Mittelstadt (2019) argues that AI development lacks the proven methods that medicine uses to translate principles into practice. Morley et al. (2020) describe the debate as focused on the "what" of AI ethics rather than the "how", and note that the AI community's ability to act on ethical risks "is still at its infancy" (p. 2141). In a scoping review, Sadek et al. (2025) conclude that responsible AI guidelines "currently fail to impact practice" (p. 199) and group the reasons into five areas: the abstract nature of guidelines, the difficulty of selecting and reconciling values, the difficulty of defining success metrics, the fragmentation of the AI development pipeline, and the lack of internal advocacy and accountability. These match the problems reported by practitioners in Sections 2.1 and 2.2.

Australia's policy has moved in a way that responds directly to this criticism. The 2019 Principles are statements of value, such as "AI systems should be inclusive and accessible" (Department of Industry, Science and Resources [DISR], 2019). The 2025 *Guidance for AI Adoption* is instead written as six actions: decide who is accountable, understand impacts and plan accordingly, measure and manage risks, share essential information, test and monitor, and maintain human control (DISR, 2025a). Several of these map onto the barriers in the literature. "Decide who is accountable" addresses the diffusion of responsibility found by Sanderson et al. (2023) and the lack of internal accountability identified by Sadek et al. (2025). "Test and monitor" and "measure and manage risks" turn abstract goals such as reliability into checkable activities. The guidance also comes in two versions, one for organisations starting out and one for governance and technical professionals, which answers the complaint that principles are not written for the people who must apply them.

This is a real improvement in design, but two cautions apply. First, there is not yet independent evidence that the Guidance changes behaviour. The Responsible AI Index data predates it, and no evaluation has been published. Second, clearer instructions do not remove the organisational pressures described in Section 2.2. A practice such as "maintain human control" can still be skipped when it slows a product launch. Better guidance narrows the gap between principle and practice, but does not close it on its own.

### 2.4 Voluntary guidance without enforcement

The final issue is whether any voluntary instrument can be effective without consequences for ignoring it. Hagendorff's (2020) central claim is that ethics "lacks mechanisms to reinforce its own normative claims" (p. 99). Munn (2023) goes further and calls AI ethics principles "useless". He describes them as meaningless because they are contested, isolated because industry and education largely ignore ethics, and toothless because they "lack consequences and adhere to corporate agendas" (p. 869). Both authors also warn that voluntary principles can delay binding rules by suggesting that self-regulation is sufficient.

Recent Australian policy makes this concern concrete. In 2024 the government proposed mandatory guardrails for AI in high-risk settings (DISR, 2024). The National AI Plan released in December 2025 did not proceed with standalone mandatory guardrails. It relies instead on existing technology-neutral laws, voluntary guidance and a new advisory AI Safety Institute (DISR, 2025b). The European Union took the opposite path. Its AI Act imposes binding obligations on high-risk systems, with fines of up to 7% of global annual turnover for the most serious breaches (Regulation 2024/1689). In Australia, therefore, the effectiveness of the Principles and the Guidance rests almost entirely on voluntary uptake, which is the mechanism that the evidence in Sections 2.1 and 2.2 shows to be weakest.

Munn's conclusion that principles are useless is too strong, however. Jobin et al. (2019) show that guidelines have produced broad international agreement on a core set of values. That agreement gives regulators a shared vocabulary to build on, and the risk-based obligations of the EU AI Act cover much the same ground as the earlier principles. Existing Australian privacy and consumer laws also apply to AI (DISR, 2025b), so voluntary guidance does not operate in a legal vacuum. Voluntary guidance can also be updated faster than legislation, as the move from ten guardrails to six practices within about a year shows. The more defensible conclusion is that principles are necessary but not sufficient. They define what good practice looks like, but they change behaviour reliably only when organisations have a reason to follow them, whether that reason comes from leadership, market pressure or law.
